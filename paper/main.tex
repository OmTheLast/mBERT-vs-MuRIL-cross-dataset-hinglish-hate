\title{Cross-Dataset Evaluation of mBERT and MuRIL for\\
Hinglish and Hindi-English Harmful Speech Detection}
\author{Om Patnaik\\\small Independent Researcher}
\date{November 16, 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Hindi-English harmful-speech datasets differ in script, topic, platform,
and annotation policy. We compare multilingual BERT (mBERT) and
Indian-language-focused MuRIL across a Kaggle Hinglish hate subset,
CM code-mixed offensive tweets, and THAR targeted religious hate.
Across three seeds, matched macro F1 is $67.5\pm2.1$ versus $58.1\pm5.7$
on Kaggle, $77.7\pm1.9$ versus $76.1\pm2.3$ on CM, and $74.7\pm0.1$
versus $76.5\pm1.3$ on THAR for mBERT and MuRIL, respectively.
The reported matched evaluation splits also select the best epoch;
these are selection-set scores rather than untouched final-test estimates.
Exploratory single-seed experiments reveal large transfer gaps,
competitive TF-IDF baselines, and mixture-dependent training failures.
MuRIL predicts only the negative class under two mixtures at the default
threshold, but not under the other two. Threshold diagnostics distinguish
compressed positive scores from nearly constant predictions.
Neither model has the highest observed score in every dataset condition.
The study does not isolate causal effects of script or label policy,
and the small CM and THAR differences are not established as statistically
reliable. The results support dataset-specific evaluation and cautious
interpretation of cross-dataset model comparisons.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Hinglish commonly combines English and Hindi, including Romanized Hindi,
Devanagari, and mixed-script text. Online comments add spelling variation,
slang, hashtags, and missing conversational context. A model that works on
one collection may behave differently on another, even when both
are described as hate-speech datasets.

This study began with a direct comparison: does Indian-language-focused
pretraining in MuRIL improve Hinglish hate-speech detection relative to
mBERT? The observed ranking changed across datasets. We therefore examine
how the models compare within each dataset, how scores change under
transfer, and whether combining training sources improves robustness.
We use \emph{harmful speech} as an umbrella term; the source labels hate,
offensive, and AntiReligion remain distinct tasks.

The contributions are a three-seed matched comparison, a single-seed
cross-dataset comparison against lexical baselines, and four exploratory
training mixtures with prediction and threshold diagnostics. We separate
measured outcomes from possible explanations. Our experiments compare
existing models; they do not introduce a new architecture or identify
which dataset property causes a change in ranking.

\section{Related Work}
BERT introduced bidirectional Transformer pretraining for language
understanding~\cite{devlin2019bert}. Its multilingual cased checkpoint
provides the general multilingual baseline used here. MuRIL focuses on
Indian languages and includes translated and transliterated pretraining
signals~\cite{khanuja2021muril}. This makes it a relevant comparison for
Hindi-English text, without guaranteeing better task-specific performance.

Bohra et al.\ introduced a Hindi-English code-mixed hate-speech dataset,
illustrating the need to study this language setting directly
\cite{bohra2018dataset}. THAR provides a narrower task: targeted hate
against religion in Hindi-English YouTube comments~\cite{sharma2024thar}.
Our contribution is a comparison across source datasets and label
definitions, rather than a direct reproduction of either dataset paper.

\section{Datasets}
We use three processed collections (Table~\ref{tab:data}). Their source
labels are mapped to binary values for training, but are not reannotated
under a common definition. Thus, label~1 has a different meaning in each
collection. These datasets contain Hindi-English/code-mixed material,
but their annotations and scripts do not establish that every row mixes
both languages or that every source represents the same Indian context.

\paragraph{Kaggle Hinglish.}
The source is Shardul Dhekane's Code-Mixed Hinglish Hate Speech Detection
Dataset~\cite{dhekane2024kaggle}. The local full file has 29,550 English,
Hindi, and Hinglish rows. Controlled experiments use the deduplicated
Hinglish subset of 4,780 rows, all Latin script under our script audit.
The original collection and annotation process and its precise geographic
coverage are not fully established in the available local metadata.

\paragraph{CM code-mixed offensive text.}
We use the code-mixed subset of the source split files from the
\texttt{cm-hate-speech-detection} repository~\cite{cmrepo2024}. The text
concerns Indian politics on Twitter/X. The source \texttt{offense} label
defines the positive class. In the processed collection, 88.7\% of rows
are Latin-only, 11.0\% mix Latin and Devanagari, and 0.3\% are
Devanagari-only. Duplicate text and some conflicting labels remain a
limitation of the source collection.

\paragraph{THAR targeted religious hate.}
THAR contains Hindi-English YouTube comments annotated for targeted hate
against religion~\cite{sharma2024thar,tharrepo2024}. We use the binary
\texttt{AntiReligion}/\texttt{Non-AntiReligion} task. The processed
collection is 76.9\% Latin-only, 18.9\% Devanagari-only, and 4.2\%
mixed Latin--Devanagari. THAR is primarily Latin script too; its higher
MuRIL score does not prove a general Devanagari advantage.

\paragraph{Excluded diagnostic probe.}
A separate 79-row probe was used during early debugging. Its provenance
is uncertain, and its duplicate rows and broad labels limit interpretation.
It is excluded from the primary tables and conclusions.

\section{Experimental Method}
\subsection{Models, preprocessing, and metrics}
We fine-tune \texttt{bert-base-multilingual-cased} and
\texttt{google/muril-base-cased} as two-class classifiers. Training uses
minimal URL and username normalization, two epochs, learning rate
$2\times10^{-5}$, weight decay 0.01, batch size 8, and maximum sequence
length 128. Runs use PyTorch and Hugging Face Transformers, with Apple
Silicon MPS wrappers; CPU and CUDA wrappers are also provided. Two epochs
are a shared compute-budget choice, not an optimized stopping point.

Lexical baselines use TF-IDF with logistic regression or a linear support
vector machine. We report macro F1, positive recall, and selected error
rates. Macro F1 gives equal weight to the two class F1 scores. Positive
recall is $\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})$; its complement is the
false-negative rate. Scores refer to agreement with dataset annotations.
Unless stated otherwise, neural predictions use the largest softmax
probability, equivalent to a positive-class threshold of 0.50.

\subsection{Matched comparison and epoch selection}
Matched experiments train and evaluate within one dataset. Seeds 7, 13,
and 42 are used for each model. Kaggle and THAR use stratified 80/20
splits, with the same seed controlling split membership and training
randomness. Both models receive the same split within a seed. Their
standard deviations therefore combine split variation and training
variation. Kaggle has 3,824 training and 956 evaluation rows; THAR has
9,239 and 2,310. CM uses the fixed source train+validation portions
(3,476 rows) for training and the source test portion (424 rows) for
evaluation across all seeds.

\textbf{Selection-set limitation.} The Trainer evaluates after each epoch
and selects the checkpoint with the highest macro F1 on the reported
evaluation split. These matched scores therefore reuse evaluation data
for model selection. The CM portion named \emph{test} by its source is also
used for epoch selection here. None of these matched scores is an estimate
on an untouched final test set.

\subsection{Transfer and mixed training}
Transfer uses saved seed-42 checkpoints and evaluates them on the other
source evaluation portions. The earlier transfer harness removes duplicate
evaluation text; CM consequently has 415 evaluation rows rather than the
424 in the matched training summaries. Its matched reference scores are
computed through that harness. We keep this single-seed matrix separate
from the three-seed selection-set table.

For mixed training, the builder takes only each source's designated
training portion: seed-42 stratified training rows for Kaggle and THAR,
and source train+validation rows for CM. It concatenates and shuffles
these rows without source balancing or semantic relabeling. The input
pool sizes are 7,300 for Kaggle+CM, 13,063 for Kaggle+THAR, 12,715 for
CM+THAR, and 16,539 for all three. The training runner then makes an
internal 80/20 training/selection split of each pool. Designated source
evaluation rows are excluded from the pool by split membership; duplicate
text across portions remains a separate integrity concern. Each mixture
is evaluated separately under each source dataset's label definition.
Mixed training is single-seed exploratory evidence, not a matched-budget
ablation of source identity or label policy.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Three-seed matched comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:matched} and Figure~\ref{fig:matched} show the repeated
matched results. mBERT has the higher observed macro F1 on Kaggle and
CM; MuRIL has the higher observed value on THAR. The Kaggle gap is the
largest. CM and THAR differences are small, and no confidence intervals
or significance tests establish reliable superiority.

\section{Error and Threshold Analysis}
\subsection{Measured false negatives}
The earlier seed-42 matrix gives matched false-negative rates of 61.4\%
for mBERT and 77.7\% for MuRIL on Kaggle; 31.3\% and 44.9\% on CM;
and 22.3\% and 19.7\% on THAR. Kaggle-to-THAR rates rise to 84.1\%
and 91.8\%. These are individual-run outcomes, not three-seed means.
They support a dataset-specific comparison of missed positive labels.
Script and keyword associations in the error reports remain descriptive;
the study does not separate script from topic or label policy.

\subsection{Automated error tags}
A rule-based script tags a 285-row selected error sample using keywords,
script flags, length, and source identity. These are automated heuristic
tags, not independently human-reviewed annotations. The historical tag
\texttt{cross\_dataset\_label\_mismatch} is assigned to every transfer
row. Its 191 occurrences (67.0\% of the sample) therefore describe the
sample's composition and cannot show that label mismatch caused 67.0\%
of errors. The other tags are candidate cues for human review. No
validated causal error taxonomy is claimed.

\subsection{Threshold diagnostics and prediction collapse}
For Kaggle+CM MuRIL, positive scores lie below 0.50, with a maximum
near 0.466 in the diagnostic evaluations. A threshold of 0.42 selected
on the reconstructed internal validation portion recovers positive
predictions (Table~\ref{tab:threshold}). Thus, all-negative default
predictions do not by themselves show that no useful ranking signal
was learned. By contrast, CM+THAR MuRIL produces almost constant positive
scores near 0.4456; validation threshold selection retains 0.50 and
does not recover the same behavior.

The diagnostic script reconstructs validation splits from cleaned,
deduplicated rows, so these metrics need not equal the Trainer's saved
selection metrics. Threshold searches optimized directly on an evaluation
set are diagnostic only. We also exclude matched Kaggle-only threshold
improvements from independent test claims because their reconstructed
validation split overlaps the reported Kaggle evaluation split. Threshold
adjustment trades missed positives against false alarms and is not a
general remedy for poor transfer.

\section{Discussion}
The observed model ranking depends on the dataset condition. mBERT's
largest advantage occurs on Kaggle, where MuRIL has lower positive
recall. MuRIL's higher THAR scores and CM--THAR transfer scores are
consistent with possible alignment between its pretraining and the
target-domain language. This is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated mechanism.
Kaggle and CM are predominantly Latin script, but so is THAR; dataset-level
comparisons cannot establish that either model is better for a script.

Likewise, binary label mapping does not make hate, offense, and religious
hate interchangeable. Their differences may contribute to transfer
failure alongside platform, topic, text length, vocabulary, source balance,
and optimization. The present design varies these factors together.
Mixture-specific MuRIL failures and partial threshold recovery show why
default-threshold F1 alone is not a complete account of model behavior.

For practical use, the results favor evaluating candidate models against
the intended moderation policy and data source, with separate model
selection and final evaluation. These experiments do not assess live
deployment, moderator workload, or effects on affected communities.

\section{Limitations and Responsible Use}
The main evaluation weakness is reuse of matched evaluation data for
best-epoch selection. Three seeds provide a limited stability check,
and Kaggle/THAR seeds also change the split. Transfer, mixture, and
threshold results remain single-seed exploratory evidence. We report
no significance tests or confidence intervals and do not claim the small
CM or THAR gaps are reliable population-level differences. Different
training-pool sizes, no source balancing, and limited hyperparameter
search further restrict causal comparisons.

Annotation policies differ; Kaggle's source and geographic metadata are
incomplete; CM contains duplicate text and some label conflicts. The
earlier deduplicated evaluation harness differs from the matched training
summaries. Automated tags are not human error annotations, and the
79-row probe is excluded. These issues limit generalization to Indian
Hinglish moderation beyond the particular collections studied.

Dataset access is not equivalent to redistribution permission. Our
documented October 4, 2026 source check found an MIT field in Kaggle
metadata and no GitHub-detected license in the CM and THAR repositories.
This is a dated metadata check, not a legal determination of rights to
all underlying comments. Source citations and permission notes are
retained; this paper does not establish permission to redistribute raw
CM or THAR text. Harmful-language examples should be minimized and
contextualized in any later human review. Model outputs should not be
treated as authoritative judgments about a person or community.

\section{Conclusion}
Under the current three-seed matched protocol, mBERT has higher observed
macro F1 on Kaggle and narrowly on CM, while MuRIL has higher observed
macro F1 on THAR. Single-seed transfer, lexical baselines, and mixed-source
training show that this ranking is not stable across evaluation conditions.
Threshold diagnostics reveal both recoverable conservative predictions
and more severe nearly constant outputs. These findings support a
conditional comparison of existing models, with an explicit selection-set
limitation. Stronger evidence requires an untouched final evaluation,
repeated transfer and mixture runs, controlled script and label-policy
comparisons, and independently reviewed error examples.
